Bottom line

Westmill's project-based model and favorable deposit terms make it structurally working-capital-light. Customers fund roughly 60% of the receivables-and-inventory investment before vendors are paid, so net working capital absorbs only about 6.8% of sales after deposits — well below the 15–25% typical of capital-equipment manufacturers. Combined with a debt-free balance sheet and an average current ratio above 8x, the company funds its growth internally and carries minimal liquidity risk.

Operating NWC: Before vs. After Customer Deposits

Deferred revenue (customer deposits) routinely offsets most of the operating working capital tied up in receivables and inventory. In October, deposits fully covered the investment — NWC went negative (customers funded the business).

Liquidity: Cash & Current Ratio

Cash strengthened through the year as projects shipped and deposits converted. Current ratio expanded from ~2.2x to over 8x with no debt on the balance sheet.

Assessment & Observations

Customer-funded growth model. The 30%-at-order deposit structure, combined with securing funds before vendor payment, means customers finance roughly 60% of the receivables-and-inventory investment. Average deferred revenue of $1.99M offsets a $3.11M gross operating investment, leaving net working capital of just $1.12M — only 6.8% of sales.
Project lumpiness is visible but well-managed. Working capital swings with the project cycle — September saw AR peak at $5.4M (large project billings) against $5.4M of deposits, while October's deposits actually exceeded the operating investment, producing negative net working capital (customers funding the business outright). These swings are a feature of the model, not a stress signal, because deposits move in step with the receivables.
Conservative liquidity, zero leverage. The balance sheet carries no debt, an average $4.08M cash position, and a current ratio that strengthened from ~2.2x to over 8x across the year. Even at the September working-capital peak, liquidity was never in question.
Efficiency is solid for the sector. A combined 81-day cash conversion cycle (42 DSO + 48 DIO − 10 DPO) compresses to roughly 36 days once customer deposits are accounted for. DPO of only 10 days is a deliberate choice — there is unused capacity to extend vendor terms if the company ever wanted to free up additional cash.
Implication for capacity & growth. Because incremental projects are largely self-funding through deposits, Westmill can scale revenue without a corresponding step-up in external working capital. This is a meaningful strategic advantage and a point worth emphasizing in any buyer/PE narrative: growth does not consume cash the way it does for most capital-equipment manufacturers.
